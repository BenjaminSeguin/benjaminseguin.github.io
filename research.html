Research

Optimization on structured nonconvex landscapes

How much can a first-order method guarantee when the problem is nonconvex — and what does the geometry of the landscape have to give us in return?

The question

Nonconvex optimization is the backbone of modern machine learning, and many data science tasks — matrix completion, robust PCA, phase retrieval — are naturally nonconvex. For an objective $f\colon\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, finding a first-order stationary point is not enough: such a point may be a saddle or even a local maximum. The meaningful target is an approximate second-order stationary point (SOSP), a point $x$ with

$$\|\nabla f(x)\|\le\varepsilon_g\qquad\text{and}\qquad \lambda_{\min}\!\left(\nabla^2 f(x)\right)\ge-\varepsilon_H.$$

Second-order methods reach such a point with strong guarantees, but they need Hessian information that is often out of reach in high dimension. Perturbed first-order methods reach one with high probability using gradients alone. My work asks which practically-used first-order methods can be brought into that framework, and what the landscape must satisfy for the argument to close.

Escaping saddle points with conjugate gradient

Nonlinear conjugate gradient (NCG) is among the most widely used first-order methods, and it is observed to work well on nonconvex regression problems — but no second-order complexity guarantee was available for it. In work with Clément Royer, we close that gap with PRNCG-HB, a method built from two independent phases.

  • Descent phase. A restarted NCG iteration with a backtracking line search, safeguarded so that the conjugate direction is discarded whenever it fails a sufficient-decrease test.
  • Escape phase. When the gradient falls below $\varepsilon_g$, the iterate becomes an SOSP candidate. The method perturbs it, runs a fixed window of heavy-ball steps at constant momentum $\gamma<\tfrac12$ with the safeguard suspended, and tests the resulting decrease in $f$. Sufficient decrease certifies a strict saddle and hands control back to the descent phase; its absence certifies an $(\varepsilon_g,\varepsilon_H)$-SOSP with high probability.

Under Lipschitz continuity of the gradient and the Hessian alone, the method returns such a point in

$$\underbrace{\frac{\Delta_f}{c_{\mathcal{R}}}\,\varepsilon_g^{-2}}_{\text{restart steps}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\Delta_f}{c_{\mathcal{N}}}\,\varepsilon_g^{-\max\{1+p,\;2(1+p-q)\}}}_{\text{conjugate steps}}\;+\;\underbrace{\widetilde{O}\!\left(\frac{L_1L_2^2\Delta_f}{\varepsilon_H^{4}}\right)}_{\text{escape windows}}$$

iterations. The gradient-descent rate $\varepsilon_g^{-2}$ is charged only to the steps where the safeguard forces a restart; the exponent of the second term is a free parameter of the safeguard and can be pushed strictly below $2$. So on its descent phase the method is not bounded below by the gradient-descent rate — though the worst case retains that term. Under the single-tolerance coupling $\varepsilon_H=\sqrt{L_2\varepsilon_g}$ the escape term dominates and the bound becomes $\widetilde{O}(\varepsilon_g^{-2})$, matching perturbed gradient descent.

Adapting the perturbation argument to a heavy-ball window needs work that gradient descent does not: the linear recursion driving the difference of two nearby trajectories is governed by a non-symmetric matrix that does not diagonalize in the eigenbasis of the Hessian.

The escape phase uses nothing from the descent phase beyond the small-gradient trigger, so any scheme providing a per-step decrease could occupy it. Under the additional assumption that $f$ satisfies the strict saddle property, the certified point lies in a strongly convex basin, from which a local phase converges to a local minimizer at a rate set by the same exponents.

Numerics

On two strict-saddle instances the comparison against perturbed accelerated gradient descent is mixed when measured in iterations, but the method leads on both instances when measured in oracle evaluations, where the accelerated step costs four gradients to our one. The experiments also make clear that the constants prescribed by the analysis are far too conservative to be executed directly — a gap between guarantee and practice that I would like to narrow.

Strict saddle landscapes

The guarantees above are only as useful as the landscape assumptions behind them. Several non-equivalent notions of the strict saddle property circulate in the literature, each with parameters that behave like a strong-convexity modulus. Establishing which of them hold for concrete estimation problems — in particular robust regression with biweight losses, which first-order methods handle easily in practice without any known landscape justification — is the other half of my current work, and the part that connects the analysis back to problems people actually solve.

Toward the thesis

My PhD, in the MMSID team at LIGM, continues in this direction and widens it toward geometry and imaging. The subject is not yet fixed, but the threads I expect to pull on are:

  • Complexity guarantees for first-order and momentum methods beyond the Euclidean setting — on manifolds and other structured search spaces.
  • Landscape analysis of inverse problems in imaging, where nonconvexity is intrinsic to the forward model rather than incidental.
  • The interaction between learned regularizers and the convergence guarantees of the optimization schemes that use them.
